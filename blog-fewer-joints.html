Why fewer joints make a longer-lasting tank

Every weld is a seam where a tank can fail. The surest way to make equipment last is to design out the joints it does not need.

Plasto-Fab team · 2 min read

Where tanks fail

A thermoplastic sheet arrives from the mill with the same properties all the way through. A weld is different. It is made in the shop, by a person and a machine, and its strength depends on preparation, temperature, pressure and time. That is why, when a tank or vessel starts to leak after years in service, the leak is usually found at a joint, and rarely in the middle of a sheet.

Joints also gather stress. Nozzles, corners and seams are where the loads from the liquid, the supports and changes in temperature come together.

Designing joints out

It has been a working rule at Plasto-Fab since 1992: design out every joint you can, and weld the rest to the standard. In practice, that means:

  • Using the largest sheets the design allows, so a shell is rolled from fewer panels.
  • Placing the seams that remain away from nozzles, supports and other high-stress points.
  • Assembling large sections in our works, so fewer joints are made in the field, where conditions are harder to control.
In shortEach joint avoided is one less place for a leak to start, and one less weld to inspect.

Welding the joints that remain

Every joint we do make is welded to DVS 2207–2208, the German Welding Society's guidelines for welding thermoplastics and for the machines that do it, on German Wegener & Widos equipment.

What it means for you

Fewer joints mean fewer places for a leak to start, fewer welds to inspect, and equipment that stays in service longer between repairs. It is the same tank on your drawing, built to last.
